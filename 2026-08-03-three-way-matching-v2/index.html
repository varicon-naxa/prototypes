Overview

Three documents, one question: should we pay this?

A supplier bill is a claim. On its own it proves nothing — it is the supplier's assertion about what they sent and what it costs. Three-way matching answers the claim with two independent records that Varicon already holds: what we authorised, and what the site crew actually received. When all three agree, the bill is safe to pay. When they disagree, the disagreement itself is the useful output.

◆ Why the direction matters

The match is not a three-way average — it is a one-way test. Rate comes from the PO, quantity comes from the docket, and the bill is checked against the product of those two. Getting this backwards (letting the bill set the rate because it "looks close") is what makes matching theatre instead of control.

The rule, stated once

Expected value of a bill line
PO unit rate × Σ docket quantities = Expected amount
Every flag in the engine is a comparison against that one number. price variance is billed amount minus expected amount. qty variance is billed quantity minus summed docket quantity. off-PO means no PO line to take a rate from. missing-docket means no docket quantity to multiply by. There is no fifth kind of problem.

Tolerance is a customer setting, not a product constant

There is no single right threshold, because there is no single customer appetite. Some want a hard gate — anything off the PO stops and gets looked at. Others want flow, and would rather absorb a $20 fuel levy than have an AP officer open a bill for it. Both are legitimate positions about risk, not correctness.

So the deliverable is not a number — it is a configuration surface, a defensible default, and onboarding guidance. VDP-139 already says thresholds are "configurable per tenant"; the work is making that setting comprehensible to someone choosing it on day one, who has no variance data yet.

◆ What the match takes from here

Only two things: the unit rate per line, and the order quantity as a ceiling for over-receipt checks. Cost centre and accounting code ride along as coding defaults — though see the open question below on whether that default should come from the PO at all.

POs can now be created by scanning a quote

VDP-118 lets a user drop a supplier quote and have the PO built from it — supplier, line items, units and rates parsed out (VDP-122) using the same OCR service the bill side reuses (VDP-121). For matching this cuts both ways, and the second way is easy to miss.

↑ It improves match coverage

The biggest cause of an unmatchable bill is no PO was ever raised — because raising one was friction. Scanning a quote removes most of that friction, so more spend arrives with an authority leg attached.

It should also raise the clean-match rate directly: when the PO's rates were parsed from the supplier's own quote, the supplier's later bill is far more likely to agree with them to the cent. Quote-derived POs and bill matching are complementary in a way worth measuring — clean-match rate segmented by PO origin is a good early metric.

↓ It puts OCR inside the authority leg

Everything on this page argues the PO is the rate authority because it is human-approved and locked. If its rates were machine-read, that claim gets weaker — and the failure is silent.

If OCR reads a quote's $2,200 as $220, or transposes a rate between two lines, the PO is wrong from birth. The supplier then bills the correct figure — and three-way matching flags it as a price variance against the erroneous PO. The AP officer is now investigating the right bill instead of the wrong PO.

!
Three-way matching only checks internal consistency — it cannot detect a wrong PO
The match tests bill against PO and docket. It has no view on whether the PO faithfully reflects what was actually agreed. A quote misread at PO creation produces a PO, docket and bill that are mutually inconsistent in a way that looks like supplier overcharging, or — worse, if the docket qty happens to line up — a perfectly clean match at the wrong price. Bill OCR already fabricates line items in production (VDP-1334); the same parser now writes the authority leg.
◆ Mitigations, in order of value

1 · PO approval is the control point, and should be treated as one. It is the only human gate between OCR and a locked rate. Approvers should see OCR-derived rates marked as such — the provenance chip pattern already used elsewhere — rather than as ordinary typed values indistinguishable from verified ones.

2 · Keep the quote attached and reachable from the match screen. When a price variance is flagged, "compare against the original quote" should be one click, not a hunt through File Manager. That makes wrong-PO the first hypothesis an AP officer can cheaply test, instead of the last.

3 · Store rate provenance on the PO line so the engine can say something more useful than "price variance": "billed rate differs from a PO rate that was OCR-derived and never manually edited". That points at the likely culprit. Cheap — one field, set at creation.

▲ Open decision · cost centre source of truth

CQ-2680 and AKJ Services (Pylon #13061) both ask that cost centre default from the docket, not the PO — because the crew on site knows which cost centre the concrete actually went into, and a single PO often feeds several. No decision has been recorded either way. This prototype defaults from the docket where a docket exists, and falls back to the PO where one doesn't. It is arguably a prerequisite for VDP-134, not a follow-up.

✖ Live bug that corrupts this leg

PICBR-912 — editing a delivery docket's received quantity silently overwrites the PO's order quantity. If the authority leg can be rewritten by the leg it is supposed to check, the match proves nothing. This has to be fixed before the engine can be trusted; it was last seen Paused at High priority.

Two things worth noticing

Σ
One PO line can gather quantity from many dockets
A 40 m³ PO line might be filled by five pours over three weeks. expected_qty is the sum across every docket linked to that line — which is why a bill covering three dockets still matches cleanly.
＋
Dockets can carry items that aren't on the PO
The wash-out fee is real, was incurred, and was never ordered. Capturing it here is what lets an off-PO bill line auto-match later instead of landing on the AP officer as a mystery charge. It needs its own cost centre, since there's no PO line to inherit from.
◆ Over-receipt is a warning, not a block

The only over-delivery control that exists today is a soft prompt when delivered qty exceeds ordered qty (PICBR-1577). Deliberate: over-delivery is usually legitimate on civil jobs, and blocking the docket would just push the crew to record nothing. The check belongs at bill approval, where someone can accept it — not on a phone in a concrete truck.

✖ Live bug · unapproved POs are linkable

PICBR-1565 — dockets on POs that haven't cleared approval stay selectable, so a bill can be matched against unauthorised spend. CQ-2709 asks for this to be blocked at docket creation.

◆ Keep this independent of the tolerance posture

It is tempting to bundle this into Strict/Balanced/Flexible, but the combinations customers actually want cut across: flexible on cents, strict on evidence is arguably the smartest setting a builder can choose — absorb the $20 fuel levy without a second thought, but never pay for concrete nobody saw arrive. Bundling forces them to accept loose evidence rules to get loose price rules. Posture should pre-select this, and then it stays independently changeable.

▲ Still open · the escalation path, and scope

Default for existing tenants: attestation, not required. Switching matching on and immediately blocking every un-docketed bill would read as an outage.

Does it need narrower scope? Plausibly per project — a big contract with a dedicated site admin can sustain "required" while small maintenance jobs cannot. Same recommendation as tolerance: ship tenant-wide, leave room in the model, don't build the UI until asked twice.

Worth remembering why this matters at all: PICBR-1495 shows un-docketed bill lines don't just get paid, they silently drop out of Daily Cost Tracking — paid and invisible. Even "warn only" is a strict improvement on today.

The ambiguity case, which has no clean answer

?
Two PO lines share a rate — which one does this bill line belong to?
If 32 MPa and 40 MPa concrete are both priced at $220/m³ and the bill says only "concrete supply", matching on rate is genuinely ambiguous. The engine must not pick one silently: the two lines have different cost centres and different remaining order quantities, so a coin-flip misallocates cost. Return both candidates and make the user choose — the same instinct VDP-140 applies to PO inference.

Rate variance is asymmetric

Billed above the PO rate is a commercial problem — someone is being charged more than was agreed. Billed below is not; it is a discount, and blocking it wastes everyone's time.

So the tolerance should almost certainly be one-sided: flag overs, pass unders. The Sandbox has a toggle for this because it is a real choice — symmetric tolerance is defensible if you care about data quality and want to know when a supplier's pricing drifts either way.

Also relevant: supplier rates are truncated at 2 decimal places internally, causing per-cent discrepancies on large quantities (PICBR-1184). A rounding allowance isn't optional — without one, a 10,000-unit line fails on arithmetic noise.

Consume dockets, not just PO lines

The modelling decision that matters

Consumption has to be tracked at docket-line granularity, not just as a total per PO line. Otherwise you cannot answer "which of these dockets is still available?" — which is exactly what VDP-134's linking modal needs in order to filter already-billed dockets out.

And it must support partial consumption. An 8 m³ docket with 5 m³ billed should reappear offering 3 m³, not vanish and not reappear whole. VDP-141's proposed schema stores matched_docket_ids[] — a flat list, which cannot express "3 of 8 remaining". That needs to become a through-model carrying a quantity.

▲ Open decision · do draft bills reserve docket quantity?

Two AP officers open two draft bills against the same docket at the same time. Both see 8 m³ available; both claim it; both get approved. Nothing in VDP-141 prevents this.

Option A — consume on approval only. Simple, and matches how the rest of Varicon behaves, but allows the race. Needs a re-validation at the approval boundary that can fail an already-approved-by-tier-1 bill, which is a confusing user experience.

Option B — soft reservation on draft. Draft claims mark quantity reserved and show "3 m³ available (5 m³ on draft bill INV-…)". More honest, and cheap in Django — but requires reservation release on draft delete, and a staleness sweep for abandoned drafts.

Recommendation: B for visibility, A for enforcement — reservations are advisory in the UI, and the authoritative consumption check runs inside the approval transaction. That way the race is impossible without drafts being able to permanently lock quantity.

✖ Credit notes invert the ledger and are unmodelled

A credit note reduces previously-billed quantity, so it must return consumption to the pool. Nothing in VDP-134 or VDP-139 mentions credit notes, and OCR currently misreads them as invoices (VDP-694 — being fixed before release) — which would add consumption instead of removing it, then block the correct re-bill as over-consumed. Signed quantities are necessary but not sufficient: whether a credit releases quantity at all depends on why it was issued. See the credit-note section — getting this wrong opens a duplicate-payment route straight through the feature.

Model · what is matchable

Scope — the evidence leg is polymorphic

Everything up to here assumed the evidence leg is a delivery docket: goods arriving in discrete drops. External plant hire and ABN labour both break that, and not superficially — plant accrues over time with no delivery event, and labour is evidenced by an approved timesheet. The right answer is not three parallel workflows. It is one engine that does not know or care which kind of evidence it is reading.

Why the engine shouldn't know the difference

The comparison is identical in all three cases

Authorised rate × evidenced quantity = expected amount. That holds whether the quantity is 8 m³ of concrete, 37 hours of excavator, or 76 hours of a carpenter's time. The units differ; the arithmetic does not.

So the engine stays exactly as specified in VDP-139. What changes is only where the evidenced quantity comes from — and that belongs behind an abstraction, not inside the matching logic.

This is a schema decision, not a later feature

The allocation ledger must point at a generic evidence line, not at DocketLine specifically. Build it docket-only and adding plant hire means migrating live financial allocation data — exactly the migration worth avoiding.

Cost of doing it now: one extra table and a source discriminator. Cost of doing it later: a data migration over rows that money has already flowed through.

Model · the inverse case

Evidence without authority — dockets captured, no PO

Someone ordered concrete over the phone because the pour couldn't wait. The crew captured the docket. The bill has now arrived and there is no PO anywhere. This is the opposite failure to the one the walkthrough covers, and it is far more common than "PO but no docket" — because a delivery that nobody authorised still gets delivered, and the crew still does their job.

◆ Retrospective PO conversion is the highest-value action on this screen

It should be a single button, pre-filled from the bill, not a "go and create a PO" instruction that sends the user to another module and loses their place. ▲ Per-client setting · are no-PO bills permitted at all?

Confirmed as a customer-level choice, consistent with the docket requirement. The options:

  • Allowed — code manually and proceed. Suits customers where phone orders are normal reality.
  • Allowed with retrospective PO — must convert before approval. Enforces discipline at the one moment the user is motivated to comply, because the bill is blocking.
  • Blocked — available, but expect it to produce backdated POs raised purely to clear bills. That is worse than allowing it: you get the same spend with fake authorisation and a false audit trail.

Default: allowed. A customer who has just switched matching on and finds half their bills blocked will conclude the feature is broken, not that their purchasing is undisciplined — even when the latter is true.

Spend type · external plant hire

Plant hire — where time on site ≠ billable time

A 20-tonne excavator arrives on Monday and leaves three weeks later. Nothing is "delivered" in between. The evidence leg has to be an affirmative record of chargeable time, and the single most important fact about plant hire is that this is not the same as elapsed time on site. That gap is where the money is.

Δ
21 days on site is not 21 days billable — and without a record you cannot argue
Three weeks on site might be 15 working days, with two rained-out days at standby rate and four weekend days not charged at all. If your only evidence is the on-hire and off-hire dates, the supplier's invoice for 21 days is unarguable — you have no independent record to contradict it. On-hire/off-hire dates are a bracket, not evidence. The daily record is the evidence.

This changes the unit-mismatch rule

Earlier this prototype says the engine must refuse to coerce mismatched units rather than silently multiply. That still holds — but a bill in days against a monthly PO rate is not a unit conflict. It is a decomposition, and it is the normal case for plant.

So distinguish two failures: convertible via the rate card (compute it, show the working) versus genuinely incommensurable — hours billed against a PO holding only a monthly rate with no hourly rate and no stated hours-per-day. The second must flag; the first must not.

◆ Dry and wet hire compute evidenced quantity differently

Dry hire is elapsed-minus-exclusions. The machine is yours for a period, so chargeable duration starts from calendar elapsed time between on-hire and off-hire, then subtracts whatever the agreement says isn't chargeable (weekends, stand-down, public holidays).

Wet hire is sum-of-hours. Chargeable duration is the total of recorded working hours; nothing is subtracted because nothing was added in the first place.

Two genuinely different computations of the same field. Worth naming in the engine (duration_basis: elapsed | attendance) rather than discovering it as a bug when the first monthly dry-hire invoice arrives.

▲ Per-client setting · does the PO quantity bind?

Confirmed as a customer-level choice, and it is the right call — plant behaves differently across customers because their commercial arrangements genuinely differ.

The better idea — don't match, generate

★
Recipient Created Tax Invoice makes the match unnecessary by construction
Under an RCTI arrangement — ATO-recognised, requires a written agreement between the parties, and already common in Australian construction — the head contractor generates the invoice from the approved timesheet at the PO rate, and the worker doesn't issue one at all. There is then nothing to reconcile: hours come from approved timesheets and the rate comes from the PO, so the document is correct the moment it exists. You have eliminated the discrepancy rather than detected it.

Why this is worth building instead of matching

Matching ABN invoices catches errors after a worker has already spent time producing a document, often badly — sole traders' invoices are a well-known source of wrong rates, wrong hours, missing ABNs and inconsistent GST treatment. Every one of those becomes an exception for AP to chase, against a person who may not respond quickly.

Generating removes the whole class. It also removes the chase-the-subbie-for-an-invoice delay that holds up payment runs, which workers themselves prefer — a rare feature where the control and the convenience point the same way.

✖ Self-approval is the real risk here, not overcharging

The whole value of the timesheet as evidence rests on someone other than the payee having approved it. A worker who approves their own timesheet and then invoices against it will produce a flawless three-way match every time — and the feature will actively vouch for it. This is worth an explicit rule: evidence approved by the payee cannot satisfy the evidence leg. Varicon already treats approver-collision as a defect class for bills (VDP-73, items 12 and 17 in the PO feedback); the same principle applies with more force here, because here it defeats a financial control rather than just skipping a step.

▲ Out of scope, but worth naming once

Whether a worker is genuinely a contractor or is an employee in substance is a legal classification question with real consequences, and it is not Varicon's to answer. What is relevant to this design: the records created here — agreed rates, approved hours, who directed the work — are exactly the records that get examined if that question is ever asked. So they should be accurate and attributable, and the product should not do anything that quietly encourages treating ABN workers identically to employees. Recording facts well is the right posture; offering guidance is not.

Timing · the biggest design gap

Matching is eventual, not instantaneous

The walkthrough implies an order: PO, then docket, then bill. Reality is unordered. Suppliers invoice same-day while crews process dockets at the end of the week, so at the moment a bill arrives the evidence frequently doesn't exist yet. If that state is reported as a discrepancy, the feature's most common output on day one is a false alarm — and users will learn to dismiss the flag that matters.

Δ
Under-evidenced and over-billed produce identical arithmetic and mean opposite things
Bill claims 8 m³, evidence shows 5 m³. That is either "the supplier is over-billing by 3 m³" or "the third docket hasn't been captured yet" — and the numbers cannot tell you which. What distinguishes them is time, and whether more evidence is expected. Treating every shortfall as a variance is the fastest way to make the feature untrustworthy.

Match state, not match result

The fix is to stop treating the match as a verdict computed once at arrival, and treat it as a state that improves as evidence lands. A bill's match status is live.

What ends the wait

✓
Evidence arrives

A new confirmed docket, plant record or timesheet triggers re-match. If it now matches cleanly, the bill moves itself into the approval queue and the AP officer is told — they never had to come back and check.

This is the payoff. Most parked bills should resolve without anyone touching them.

⏱
The window expires

After N days with no new evidence, "waiting" becomes "not coming". The shortfall converts to a real qty_variance and the bill enters the approval queue for a decision.

Per-client, default 7 days — but see the due-date constraint below, which overrides it.

⊘
The PO line is closed

A short-close is an explicit statement that no further deliveries are coming. That is the cleanest possible terminator — it converts waiting into a variance immediately, with no timer involved.

Which is why PO close and matching need designing together, not sequentially.

$
Don't park a bill past its due date — where the due date is still ahead
A parked bill is still a payable, and parking is invisible to the supplier. Waiting for a docket until day 7 on 7-day terms means paying late — trading a reconciliation problem for a supplier-relationship one. So the window is min(window, due_date − buffer), and a bill approaching its due date while parked must escalate loudly rather than expire quietly. But that formula collapses when the bill is already overdue on arrival — see below, because that is the common case, not the edge case.
130
Reality check: on a live tenant, 130 of 148 bills were already overdue
Observed tab counts: 148 bills, 130 overdue, 73 still in Draft. If ~88% of bills are past due by the time anyone processes them, then due_date − buffer is already negative for most of them and the rule above would mean nothing ever parks — every bill with incomplete evidence goes straight to a variance, and we are back to the warning fatigue the parking state exists to prevent.

Revised rule

The due date can only discipline the window when it is in the future. Split the two cases explicitly:

Due date ahead
Park for min(window, due − buffer). Escalate as the buffer closes.
Already overdue
Park for the full window, but escalate immediately and flag as "parked, already overdue" — visibly distinct from "will go overdue while parked". The bill was late before matching touched it; blocking it doesn't make it less late.

The distinction matters for blame as much as behaviour. AP must be able to see that matching didn't cause the delay.

The uncomfortable reading

73 drafts and 130 overdue says the bottleneck is upstream of approval — bills are sitting unprocessed past their due date before any matching exists. Adding a gate to a pipeline that is already not keeping up will make the symptom worse and matching will get the blame.

Which strengthens the rollout argument: launch on warn-only for a tenant like this, and treat "bills processed within terms" as a health metric to watch alongside docket coverage. A customer whose AP is already behind is not a good first candidate for enforcement, however much they need the control.

Caveat worth confirming: this may be a staging tenant with stale seeded dates rather than a production picture. If it's real, it's the most important adoption signal in this document; if it's test data, the revised parking rule stands anyway.

Model · shape of the evidence

Multi-PO dockets and partial deliveries

Two shapes the wiki doesn't document, both common on civil jobs, and both with a clear recommendation. They matter more than they look because "is more evidence coming?" is the question that ends parking — so completeness is not a reporting nicety, it's the terminator for the state machine.

Can one docket span several POs?

✓
Recommendation: yes — because the PO link belongs on the docket line, not the header
A docket is a physical piece of paper describing one truck's drop. There is no reason its contents must all belong to one PO, and in practice they often don't — a general supplier delivers fencing for one PO and formwork for another on the same run. Forcing one PO per docket makes the crew either split one delivery into two fictional dockets or mis-allocate half the load.

This already works in the evidence model

Each EvidenceLine carries its own po_line FK, so a docket producing three evidence lines against two POs needs no schema change at all. The header-level PO field is the thing that's wrong — it should be a convenience default that pre-fills the lines, not the authority.

That reframing costs nothing now. Left as-is, a header-level PO becomes load-bearing in queries and reports, and unpicking it later is the migration we keep trying to avoid.

Two guards worth having

1
Warn if a docket spans projects. Spanning POs is normal; spanning projects is almost always a mis-keyed entry, and it corrupts cost tracking on two jobs at once. Warn, don't block — shared-site deliveries do genuinely happen.
2
Block if it spans suppliers. One docket is one supplier's paperwork by definition. If two appear, the docket has been mis-entered or two dockets were photographed as one.

Also relevant: PICBR-1373 and PICBR-1557 both concern dockets and POs missing from the link modal. A line-level model makes that modal's job clearer — it's picking PO lines, not POs.

Partial deliveries — already handled, with one missing signal

A 40 m³ PO line filled by five pours over three weeks needs no special handling: expected_qty is the sum of evidence, which the design has done from the start. What's missing is knowing when the sum is final.

Tax · sync variance

GST — why totals drift on the way to Xero and MYOB

This is a separate problem from matching and worth treating as one. A bill can match perfectly inside Varicon and still land in Xero a few cents different — and because it is cents, it gets deprioritised until someone tries to reconcile a month and finds hundreds of bills each slightly wrong. There are five distinct causes and only one of them is really about rounding.

Fix 1 alone should eliminate most of what you're seeing
The line-versus-document tax base is the only cause that fires on every multi-line bill, and it is a low-effort fix: send the tax amount you calculated instead of letting the other system calculate its own. Everything downstream then agrees by construction rather than by coincidence. I'd verify this hypothesis first — take a handful of drifting bills and check whether the delta equals the line-sum-versus-document-total difference. If it does, that's your answer and the rest is refinement.

Money arithmetic, once, properly

# A single rounding policy, applied at defined boundaries only. # Decimal throughout — float has no place anywhere near money. from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_UP CENTS = Decimal("0.01") RATE_PRECISION = Decimal("0.000001") # 6dp — rates are not currency GST_RATE = Decimal("0.10") def money(value): """ATO expects rounding to the nearest cent, half up. Python's default is banker's rounding — which is NOT what you want here and is a classic source of one-cent drift against accounting systems.""" return Decimal(value).quantize(CENTS, rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP) def line_amounts(qty, unit_rate, tax_code): # Round ONCE, at the extended amount. Never round the rate itself. ex_gst = money(Decimal(qty) * Decimal(unit_rate)) tax = money(ex_gst * GST_RATE) if tax_code.applies_gst else money("0") return LineAmounts(ex_gst=ex_gst, tax=tax, inc_gst=ex_gst + tax) def from_inclusive(inc_gst, tax_code): """Convert ONCE at entry, store both. Never re-derive later — round-tripping inclusive <-> exclusive loses a cent per trip.""" if not tax_code.applies_gst: return money(inc_gst), money("0") ex_gst = money(Decimal(inc_gst) / (1 + GST_RATE)) return ex_gst, money(Decimal(inc_gst) - ex_gst) # tax is the remainder, # so ex + tax == inc exactly def bill_totals(lines): # Document totals are SUMS of already-rounded lines — never recomputed # from the document subtotal. This is the contract we send to Xero/MYOB. subtotal = sum((l.ex_gst for l in lines), Decimal("0")) tax = sum((l.tax for l in lines), Decimal("0")) return Totals(subtotal=subtotal, tax=tax, total=subtotal + tax)

The detail in from_inclusive is worth keeping: deriving tax as inc − ex rather than ex × 0.10 guarantees the three numbers reconcile exactly. Compute it the other way and you can produce a bill whose own subtotal plus GST doesn't equal its total, which is the version of this bug that customers notice fastest.

When the supplier's own total disagrees

≠
A supplier who rounded differently is a data question, not an arithmetic one
Sometimes the invoice's printed total genuinely differs from the sum of its lines by a cent or two, because the supplier's own system rounded elsewhere. You cannot fix their arithmetic, so decide explicitly what to do: accept a bounded rounding difference (say ±$0.10) recorded as such, and treat anything larger as a real discrepancy needing a human. VAR-19965 already exists for an OCR rounding-adjustment line, so the concept is in flight.
✖ Careful how you post the adjustment

The instinct is to add a small negative line — but Xero rejects negative quantities (PICBR-702), which is exactly the class of silent sync failure that makes bills sit unsynced. Two safer options: absorb the difference into the largest line's tax amount (invisible, exact, and works because you're now sending explicit per-line tax), or post to a dedicated rounding account if the tenant has one configured. Adding a line item should be the last choice, not the first.

◆ How this interacts with matching — match on GST-exclusive, always

PO rates are held ex-GST and dockets carry no tax at all, so ex-GST is the only basis all three legs share. Every comparison in the engine must run on exclusive amounts, converting an inclusive bill once on the way in. Worth stating explicitly in the spec, because the alternative — comparing an inclusive bill total against an exclusive expected amount — produces a clean, plausible, entirely wrong 10% variance on every single line, and the tolerance band is nowhere near wide enough to hide it. It would look like systematic supplier overcharging.

Resolution · waiting on the supplier

Disputes — a hold is an overlay, not a status

A bill you're arguing about currently has nowhere to sit but Awaiting Approval, so it blocks an approver who cannot action it and ages as though the delay were yours. VDP-698 proposes a hold status — but the way it should be built follows from how it needs to behave, and the answer is not a new value in the status enum.

✓
The product already does this — Overdue is an overlay, not a status
On a live Bills list: ALL 148 · DRAFT 73 · AWAITING CREDIT 1 · AWAITING APPROVAL 3 · PARTIALLY APPROVED 0 · AWAITING PAYMENT 56 · PAID 8 · OVERDUE 130 · REJECTED 7. The lifecycle statuses sum to exactly 148 — so Overdue's 130 is not one of them. It is a cross-cutting flag that happens to be rendered as a tab, and a bill is simultaneously Draft and Overdue. Disputes should follow the identical pattern: a filter over the list, not a value in the status enum. The precedent exists, users already understand it, and no new UI concept is needed.
⊕
Why an overlay rather than a status
A disputed bill stays in payables aging, keeps aging normally, and can be disputed by any approver in the chain. All three of those mean the dispute is orthogonal to where the bill is in its lifecycle — a bill can be Awaiting Approval and disputed, Partially Approved and disputed, even Overdue and disputed. Adding Disputed to the status enum forces a combinatorial explosion across nine existing statuses, breaks the status tabs, and loses the information about where the bill actually was. A Dispute record attached to the bill keeps both facts intact.

↻

Integration · rejected bill workflow

Rejection and resubmission — three kinds of waiting

The rejected-bill reapproval work already in flight (2026-07-08-rejected-bill-reapproval) solves the dead-end that PICBR-1750 describes, and its "Apply credit & resubmit" path is the back half of the dispute flow — supplier issues a credit, bill goes back through approval carrying it. These should join up rather than run as two parallel mechanisms.

This supersedes a blocker listed elsewhere in this prototype

The remaining work is making sure the two features know about each other.

The interaction that will bite — consumption on rejection

⚠
A rejected bill must stop consuming evidence, or its own correction gets blocked
If a rejected bill's allocations keep counting, the docketed quantity stays consumed. The amended bill is then blocked as over_consumed against evidence its own earlier version is holding — recreating the PICBR-1750 dead end through a completely different mechanism, and much harder to diagnose because everything looks correct.

The good news: the design already handles it

Consumption is computed with a bill__status__in=COMMITTED_STATUSES filter, so allocation rows only count when the bill is committed. Rejection changes status, and consumption releases itself — no explicit release step, nothing to forget.

Make consumption a function of status rather than a separate action and this whole class of bug disappears. But it has to be deliberate, and it needs a test.

One subtlety worth naming

Partially Approved must not count. A bill rejected at tier 3 after tiers 1 and 2 approved has been part-way through the chain. Only final approval commits consumption — otherwise a bill that never completes approval permanently holds quantity.

So COMMITTED_STATUSES is exactly Awaiting Payment and Paid — not Awaiting Approval, not Partially Approved, not Rejected. Small list, high consequence.

Resubmission must re-match, not restore

Between rejection and resubmission, four things can have changed. The stored verdict is therefore stale by default, and showing approvers the old one would be worse than showing none.

Reverse flow · credit notes

Credit notes — where quantity and value must part company

A credit note is not simply a negative bill, and treating it as one produces a subtle, expensive bug. The question that decides everything is: does this credit return the goods, or only the money? Those two cases must behave differently in the consumption ledger, and nothing in VDP-134 or VDP-139 currently distinguishes them.

!
The trap, stated plainly
If every credit note releases consumed quantity, then a supplier who over-charged on rate and issued a price-correction credit has just re-opened 8 m³ of billable headroom — for concrete that was delivered, kept, and already paid for. The supplier can now bill that quantity again and it will match cleanly. You have built a duplicate-payment route through the feature designed to prevent duplicate payments. Conversely, if no credit ever releases quantity, a cancel-and-rebill can never be re-billed and the bill is permanently stuck.

The modelling consequence

Quantity and value are separate concerns

This is the design point worth carrying into the schema. A price-correction credit is a bill line with a negative amount and an allocation quantity of zero. The ledger tracks quantity; value follows the bill lines independently.

Earlier framing in this prototype said "signed quantities handle credit notes naturally" — that is true for cancel-and-rebill and half-true elsewhere. Signed quantities are necessary but not sufficient; the type is what decides whether a quantity is written at all.

A credit note matches the bill, not the PO

A credit note's counterparty is the original bill, not the PO and dockets. So it is a two-way match on a different axis: does this credit reference a real bill, and does it credit no more than that bill charged?

That means a credit note should not run through the three-way engine at all. It runs a credit-validation path, and its effect on the ledger is what the three-way engine later reads. Conflating the two is how you end up flagging a credit note for having "no docket".

Validation rules

#RuleWhy
1A credit must not exceed what was billed — cumulative credits ≤ cumulative billed, per line. Otherwise consumption goes negative and the ledger starts inventing billable headroom out of nothing. Enforce as an invariant, not a warning.
2Released quantity can never exceed consumed quantity for that docket line. Σ allocations per docket line must stay in [0, received_qty]. The single invariant worth property-testing.
3Credit type is mandatory and cannot default. There is no safe default. Defaulting to "releases quantity" creates the duplicate-payment route above; defaulting to "doesn't" strands cancel-and-rebills. Make the user say which.
4Unlinked credits are value-only. If a credit references no bill (supplier statement credits, annual rebates), it cannot release specific quantity. Treat as off-PO value and code it manually. Never guess which bill it belongs to.
5Crediting a synced bill needs an accounting-side reversal. If the original already pushed to Xero, the credit must sync as a credit note and allocate against that invoice — partial allocation included (CQ-2696).
6Releasing quantity must not silently un-approve anything. A credit changes available headroom, so other draft bills' verdicts go stale. Mark them stale; do not recompute approved bills.
◆ Good news on sequencing

Fixing VDP-694 (credit notes read as invoices) before release is the right call and it is genuinely load-bearing here — not just an OCR accuracy issue. A credit note misclassified as an invoice adds consumption instead of removing it: the ledger records more quantity consumed than was ever delivered, the invariant in rule 2 is violated, and the correct re-bill is then blocked as over_consumed. It fails in the direction that looks like the feature is broken. Worth adding a guard even after the fix: a document whose total is negative, or which contains "credit" in its type field, should never enter the bill path.

Governance — this is a financial control

Who can change it, and what happens when they do

Widening a tolerance silently increases what auto-passes. That is materially the same act as raising someone's approval limit, and should be treated the same way: restricted permission, audit-logged, with the old and new values recorded.

Given VDP-687 and VDP-701 already exist because super-admin approval bypasses were a real problem, a tolerance field editable by anyone with settings access is the same class of hole. It should not be reachable from a general Settings page without an explicit permission.

Changes apply forward only

A tolerance change must not retroactively re-verdict bills that were already approved. The tolerance_snapshot on each verdict plus effective_from on the config row is what makes this true — and it is why the config must be versioned rather than edited in place.

Open question: what about bills currently sitting in approval when the setting changes? Recommendation: they keep the tolerance they were matched under, and re-matching on approval uses the snapshot, not the live config. Otherwise a settings change can flip a bill under an approver mid-review.

The engine as a pure function

# billing/matching/engine.py — no ORM access, no tenant lookups, no I/O. # Callers assemble the inputs; this module only decides. def match_bill_line(bill_line: BillLineInput, po_line: POLineInput | None, available_qty: Decimal, tol: Tolerance) -> LineVerdict: flags = [] if po_line is None: # no rate authority exists — quantity may still be evidenced flags.append("off_po") return LineVerdict(status="needs_coding", expected_price=None, flags=flags) expected_qty = available_qty expected_price = (po_line.unit_rate * expected_qty).quantize(CENTS) billed_amount = (bill_line.qty * bill_line.unit_rate).quantize(CENTS) qty_var = bill_line.qty - expected_qty price_var = billed_amount - expected_price if expected_qty == 0: flags.append("missing_docket") elif abs(qty_var) > tol.qty_abs: flags.append("qty_variance") # price band: greater/lesser of a % of expected and an absolute floor. # The absolute floor exists because rates truncate at 2dp (PICBR-1184) — # without it, large-quantity lines fail on arithmetic noise alone. band = (max if tol.combine == "max" else min)( expected_price * tol.price_pct / 100, tol.price_abs) breach = price_var > band if tol.direction == "over" else abs(price_var) > band if breach: flags.append("price_variance") return LineVerdict( expected_qty=expected_qty, expected_price=expected_price, qty_variance=qty_var, price_variance=price_var, flags=flags, status="matched" if not flags else "flagged", )

Keeping this ORM-free is what makes the six VDP-139 scenarios testable as a parametrised table with no database at all — which is the difference between a suite that runs in milliseconds and one nobody runs. Assemble inputs in a thin service layer above it.

Available quantity — the query that matters

# One aggregate per PO line, computed in the database, excluding this bill. # Signed quantities mean credit notes subtract naturally — no special case. from django.db.models import Sum, F, Value, DecimalField from django.db.models.functions import Coalesce def available_by_po_line(po, exclude_bill_id=None): consumed = (BillLineDocketAllocation.objects .filter(po_line__purchase_order=po, bill_line__bill__status__in=COMMITTED_STATUSES) # approved + awaiting payment .exclude(bill_line__bill_id=exclude_bill_id) .values("po_line") .annotate(qty=Coalesce(Sum("quantity"), Value(0), output_field=DECIMAL))) received = (DocketLine.objects .filter(po_line__purchase_order=po, docket__status=DocketStatus.CONFIRMED) .values("po_line") .annotate(qty=Coalesce(Sum("received_qty"), Value(0), output_field=DECIMAL))) consumed_map = {r["po_line"]: r["qty"] for r in consumed} return {r["po_line"]: r["qty"] - consumed_map.get(r["po_line"], 0) for r in received}
N
Two aggregate queries per bill, not per line
The obvious implementation walks bill lines and asks "what's available?" each time — that is an N+1 that will bite on a 40-line quarry invoice. Compute both maps once per bill and pass them into the engine. Add select_related("po_line", "docket") on the allocation fetch. At realistic volumes (a PO with a few hundred docket lines) this is a millisecond-scale query, not a performance concern.

Concurrency — where the money actually leaks

# The consumption check MUST re-run inside the approval transaction. # Anything computed at page-render time is advisory only. from django.db import transaction @transaction.atomic def approve_bill(bill, user): # Lock the PO first — a consistent lock order across all writers # prevents deadlocks between two bills touching overlapping POs. po = PurchaseOrder.objects.select_for_update().get(pk=bill.purchase_order_id) # Re-derive from locked state; never trust the draft's cached verdict. available = available_by_po_line(po, exclude_bill_id=bill.pk) verdicts = run_match(bill, po, available, tolerance_for(bill.tenant)) if any(v.status == "over_consumed" for v in verdicts.values()): # Someone else consumed the quantity while this sat in approval. raise ConsumptionConflict(verdicts) # surface, don't silently clamp persist_verdicts(bill, verdicts) bill.status = BillStatus.AWAITING_PAYMENT bill.save(update_fields=["status"])

Why lock the PO, not the docket lines

Locking individual docket lines invites deadlock: two bills claiming overlapping docket sets in different orders will each hold what the other wants. The PO is the natural aggregate root — one lock, consistent order, and contention is low in practice because two AP officers rarely process the same PO simultaneously.

If contention ever becomes real, the escape hatch is a database CHECK-style invariant enforced by a trigger on the allocation table — belt and braces, but not needed on day one.

Idempotency and retries

The UniqueConstraint on (bill_line, docket_line) makes allocation writes idempotent — a retried approval cannot double-allocate. Worth having, because the accounting sync path already retries through an SQS FIFO queue and approval side-effects will inevitably get replayed.

Do not put the sync push inside this transaction. Commit the approval, then enqueue — otherwise a slow Xero call holds a row lock on the PO.

Recomputation — resist signals

⚠
A post_save on DocketLine that recomputes matches will hurt
It is the obvious move and it goes wrong three ways: it fires during bulk imports and fixture loads, it makes a docket edit's cost unpredictable (one docket can touch many bills), and it silently mutates approved bills' verdicts — including bills already pushed to Xero. Prefer an explicit staleness marker: docket edits set match_stale = True on affected bill lines, and recomputation happens on read, on demand, or in a queued job. Stale-but-visible beats silently-rewritten. This is the gap in VDP-141's "recomputed on edit" — an edit to the docket is not an edit to the bill.

Migration and backfill

#StepNotes
1Add tables, no behaviour change Allocation + match + tolerance models ship empty. Zero risk, deployable independently.
2Write allocations on new matches only Ledger starts accumulating from a cutover date. Existing bills stay untouched.
3Backfill where the link already exists Bills with an existing docket link get allocations inferred pro-rata. Report, don't guess — anything ambiguous stays unallocated and is listed for a human.
4Accept a permanent "pre-cutover" cohort Historical bills with no line-level docket link cannot be reconstructed honestly. Mark them legacy_unmatched and exclude from consumption maths rather than inventing allocations.
5Enable the tolerance UI last Ship with the proposed defaults hard-coded, gather real variance distributions, then let tenants tune.

Step 4 is the uncomfortable one and worth stating plainly to stakeholders: for bills already in the system, Varicon cannot retrospectively prove what was double-paid. The feature prevents future duplicates; it is not a historical audit. Promising otherwise will not survive contact with the data.

Test strategy

Engine · pure

Parametrised table over the six VDP-139 scenarios plus boundary cases: variance exactly at the band, zero expected qty, negative (credit) quantities, and 4dp rounding. No DB.

Ledger · invariant

Property test: for any sequence of bill/credit/void operations, Σ allocations ≤ received_qty per docket line, always. This single invariant is the feature's correctness claim — assert it directly.

Concurrency · real DB

TransactionTestCase with two threads approving overlapping bills. One must succeed, one must raise ConsumptionConflict. Skipping this test means shipping the double-pay bug the feature exists to prevent.

▲ Sequencing recommendation

Do not build the tolerance engine first. It is the fun part and the least valuable: with tolerances at zero, exact matching already catches most of the pain. The ordering that de-risks fastest is ledger → resolution → engine → tolerances. Ship the consumption check with hard equality and the double-pay hole closes immediately; tolerances are a refinement on top, and by then you'll have real variance data to set them from instead of guessing in a meeting.
